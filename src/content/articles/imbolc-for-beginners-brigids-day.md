Imbolc falls on February 1, roughly halfway between the winter solstice and the spring equinox. It's the second festival on the Wheel of the Year after Yule, and the quieter one: the days are noticeably longer, but spring hasn't arrived. In Irish tradition it's St Brigid's Day, and in 2023 Ireland made it a public holiday.

## Imbolc at home, simply

Most of the old customs are small and domestic, which makes Imbolc an easy festival to start with:

- **Light a candle** for the returning light, the same idea as Yule but quieter.
- **Clean one room**, the original spring cleaning: clear out what the winter left behind.
- **Make a Brigid's cross.** The four-armed cross woven from rushes or straw is the best-known Imbolc craft, and instructions are easy to find.
- **Go and look for spring**: the first buds, a bird you haven't heard since autumn, the time of sunset compared with a month ago.

## Who Brigid is

Brigid appears in Irish tradition as a goddess linked with poetry, smithcraft and healing, and as St Brigid of Kildare, whose feast day is February 1. The two are tangled together in the sources, and scholars disagree about how much of the saint's story preserves the goddess. The books above take different positions on that, some closer to the sources and some more devotional.

## Worth being direct about

Much of modern Imbolc practice is a 20th-century reconstruction. What the festival involved in pre-Christian Ireland is known only in fragments, and the eight-festival Wheel of the Year itself dates from the 1950s. Brigid's association with healing is folklore, not a health practice. Treat Imbolc as a seasonal rhythm and a moment for reflection, not a ritual with effects. Never leave a candle burning unattended.

You've probably run into archetypes already: the Hero, the Trickster, the Great Mother, the Shadow. They come from the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung, and they turn up everywhere from tarot guides to screenwriting advice. Most of those uses go well beyond what Jung wrote, so it helps to know the original idea and where to read it.

## What an archetype is

Jung noticed similar figures and themes recurring in myths, religions and his patients' dreams. He proposed that people share inherited patterns of imagining and responding, which he called archetypes, and that these sit in a layer of the mind he called the collective unconscious. An archetype isn't a personality type. It's closer to a recurring theme that takes a different shape in each culture and each life.

Used that way, the idea gives you a vocabulary. "Why do I keep playing the rescuer?" is an easier question to think about once you have a name for the pattern.

## A reading order

Jung is hard to start with, and his dense volumes put a lot of people off. Go in this order and stop whenever you've had enough:

1. **A short introduction.** Stevens's book gives you the vocabulary in an afternoon or two.
2. **Jung in his own voice.** Memories, Dreams, Reflections is the easiest, and Modern Man in Search of a Soul shows how he argued.
3. **The primary sources.** Four Archetypes is short. The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious is the full version.

[Man and His Symbols](/articles/shadow-work-journaling-for-beginners/), which Jung wrote for general readers, is another good early choice; it's covered in the shadow work guide along with Robert Johnson's Owning Your Own Shadow.

## The popular books and cards

Moore and Gillette, Pearson and Myss each build their own system of named archetypes. They're easier to apply than Jung, and that's why they sell. But their lists are those authors' creations, not something Jung set out, and two people could reasonably draw up different ones. Take whatever prompts useful thought and leave the rest.

## Trying it

The simplest practice is a notebook. Pick one story you keep telling about yourself and ask which figure you're playing in it, and which one you never let yourself play. [Dream work](/articles/dream-interpretation-books/) and [shadow work journaling](/articles/shadow-work-journaling-for-beginners/) use the same approach. If you like cards as prompts, [oracle cards](/articles/oracle-cards-for-beginners/) work in a similar way.

## Worth being direct about

Archetypes and the collective unconscious are interpretive ideas, not tested science, and there's no good evidence for them as a literal inherited layer of the mind. Jung was also a person of his time, and some of what he wrote about culture and race has aged badly. None of these books or decks can tell you who you are. They're tools for reflection, and they're not diagnosis or therapy. Active imagination in particular can stir up strong material, so if you're dealing with distress or trauma, work with a licensed professional instead of on your own.

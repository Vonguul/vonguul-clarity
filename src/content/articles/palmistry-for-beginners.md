Palmistry, or hand reading, interprets the shape of the hand, the fingers and the lines of the palm. It needs less equipment than almost any practice on this site: you already carry the material with you.

## Start with your own two hands

Before buying anything, look at both of your palms side by side in good light. Most traditions read the dominant hand (the one you write with) as where you are now, and the other as what you started with. Notice the differences first, without any meanings.

Then find the three lines nearly everyone has:

- **Heart line:** across the top of the palm, below the fingers. Traditionally linked to emotions and relationships.
- **Head line:** across the middle of the palm. Traditionally linked to how you think.
- **Life line:** curving around the base of the thumb. Despite its name, most modern palmists don't read it as how long you'll live. It's usually read as vitality and major changes.

Write down what you see. A beginner book will then give you the traditional associations to compare against.

## Which book first

Richard Webster's *Palm Reading for Beginners* is the most structured start. Cassandra Eason's *A Little Bit of Palmistry* is shorter if you want to dip in first. Johnny Fincham's book is the one for people put off by mystical framing, since it drops the astrological layer older books rely on. Goldberg and Bergen's *The Art and Science of Hand Reading* is the reference to grow into.

## Reading for someone else

Palm reading is a social practice: hand someone a palm chart and they'll offer their hand. That makes it a good conversation, as long as you treat it as one. Ask the person what they think a line or shape says before you tell them. You'll learn more from their answer than from the book.

## Worth being direct about

There is no good evidence that the lines or shape of a hand predict personality, health or future events. Palm creases form before birth, partly from how the hand folds as it develops, and they change with age and use. The traditional meanings are associations built up over centuries, not findings. Two of these books, Fincham's and Goldberg and Bergen's, use the word "science" for their methods; that describes a systematic approach, not evidence that it works.

What palmistry does reliably is give you something specific to look at and talk about. Treat a reading as a prompt for reflection, never as advice about health, money or relationships.

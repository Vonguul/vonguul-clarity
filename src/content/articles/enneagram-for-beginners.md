The Enneagram sorts personality into nine types, each built around a core motivation: what a person most wants, and what they most want to avoid. Its appeal is that it describes *why* people do things, not only what they do. That makes it a good vocabulary for noticing your own habits. It is not a scientific measurement of who you are.

## The nine types, briefly

1. **The Reformer:** wants to be good and right; fears being wrong.
2. **The Helper:** wants to be loved and needed.
3. **The Achiever:** wants to succeed and be admired.
4. **The Individualist:** wants to be unique and understood.
5. **The Investigator:** wants to be capable and to understand.
6. **The Loyalist:** wants security and support.
7. **The Enthusiast:** wants freedom and new experiences; avoids pain.
8. **The Challenger:** wants control and to protect themselves.
9. **The Peacemaker:** wants peace; avoids conflict.

## Find your type for free

You don't need a paid online test. Read short descriptions of all nine types, then pick the two or three that make you uncomfortable, the ones that describe something you'd rather not admit. The type you resist is often closer than the type you'd like to be. Then ask someone who knows you well which one fits.

Hold the result loosely. If a type stops being useful, drop it.

## Worth being direct about

Research support for the Enneagram is thin and mixed. Studies of how consistently people get the same type on retesting vary, and there's little independent evidence that exactly nine distinct types exist. Mainstream personality psychology relies on trait models like the Big Five, which have far stronger evidence. Claims of being "ancient" are also overstated: the modern system was developed in the 20th century.

That doesn't make it useless. A type is a set of descriptions you can test against your own behavior, and the language of motivations can make conversations easier. Don't use it to make hiring decisions, to diagnose anyone, or to explain away behavior that hurts other people.

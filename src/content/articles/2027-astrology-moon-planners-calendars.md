A new year's planner is one of the easiest ways to build a practice into your days: the seasonal festivals and, in many of them, the moon phases are already marked, so you don't have to look them up each month. For 2027 there are two kinds worth knowing about: datebooks you carry, and wall calendars you glance at.

## Start free

If what you want is to track the new and full moons, a free moon-phase calendar does that exactly. Use it for a month or two. If you find yourself checking it often, a planner that puts the phases next to your appointments is worth having.

## Datebook or wall calendar

A datebook goes everywhere and has room to write, which suits a journaling or intention-setting practice. A wall calendar is for the household: the phases are visible to everyone, and a moon poster doubles as decor. The Llewellyn datebooks and calendars are annual editions published for decades. Their 2027 listings have short review histories because they only went on sale recently.

## For astrology, not just the moon

The Daily Planetary Guide and the Astrological Calendar go further than moon phases: planetary positions, aspects and sign changes through the year. They suit someone who already knows their way around a chart. If you're just starting, begin with [astrology for beginners](/articles/astrology-for-beginners/). The lunar cycle itself is covered in [moon phases and lunar rituals](/articles/moon-phases-lunar-rituals-for-beginners/), and the year's eight festivals in [the Wheel of the Year](/articles/wheel-of-the-year-yule-for-beginners/).

## Worth being direct about

Moon phases and planetary positions are astronomy, and every date in these calendars is accurate. What astrology says those positions mean for your week is a different matter: there's no controlled evidence that transits predict events. A planner like this works best as a rhythm for reflection, a reason to pause at each new and full moon, rather than a forecast to plan your life around.

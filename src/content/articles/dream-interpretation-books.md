Once you start remembering your dreams, the next question is what to make of them. There are three very different ways to answer it, and the books that suit you depend on which one you mean.

## 1. Look it up: symbol dictionaries

Dream dictionaries give each symbol a meaning: water, falling, teeth, a house. They're quick and fun, and that's also their limit. One symbol gets one meaning, whoever is dreaming it. Miller's 1901 list is a classic, worth reading as folklore.

## 2. Work it out: depth psychology

Freud started modern dream interpretation, and Jung broke with him: a dream's meaning comes from what its images bring up for you, not from a fixed code. Robert Johnson's Inner Work turns that into a method you can use alone. Write the dream down, list what each image reminds you of, ask which part of your life it connects to, then do something small to honor it. If this approach appeals, it fits well with [shadow work journaling](/articles/shadow-work-journaling-for-beginners/).

## 3. Understand it: the science

Sleep researchers study what dreaming is, not what a dream "means". Zadra and Stickgold's book is the clearest current account. Walker's covers sleep more broadly. Reading the science alongside the other books keeps dream work grounded.

Whichever way you go, you need the dream first: see [lucid dreaming](/articles/lucid-dreaming-getting-started/) for dream journals and building better recall.

## Worth being direct about

There's no evidence that a dream symbol has a fixed, universal meaning, or that dreams predict events. Dream dictionaries are folklore, and Freud's and Jung's theories are interpretive frameworks, not tested science. Dream work is genuinely useful as reflection: a way to notice what's on your mind. If dreams are distressing or disrupting your sleep, that's something to talk to a doctor about, not something to decode.

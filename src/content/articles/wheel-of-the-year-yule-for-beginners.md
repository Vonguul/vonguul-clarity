The Wheel of the Year is a calendar of eight seasonal festivals, spaced roughly six weeks apart. Four are astronomical: the two solstices and the two equinoxes. Four fall between them: Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasadh and Samhain. Yule, the winter solstice, is the longest night of the year, and a natural place to start.

## Yule in one evening

You don't need much to mark the solstice. The traditions that recur most often are simple: stay up through the longest night, or at least mark it, light candles for the returning sun, bring evergreens inside, and share a meal. A quiet version for one person can be as little as turning off the electric lights at sunset, lighting a candle, and writing down what you want to leave behind with the old year and what you want to grow in the next.

Check the exact date and time with the free resource above. In the northern hemisphere, the winter solstice falls around December 21.

## Build a small altar

An altar doesn't need special furniture. A cloth on a shelf or a windowsill, a pair of candles, and a few seasonal things from outside, such as pine cones, evergreen sprigs or holly, are enough. Change what's on it as the year turns. If Yule goes well, the next festival is Imbolc in early February. The box set gives you a book for each one. Year of the Witch suits people who'd rather notice what's actually happening in their own landscape than follow dates set by someone else.

## Worth being direct about

The eight-festival Wheel of the Year as it's practiced today is a modern creation. It was put together in the 1950s, largely by Gerald Gardner and Ross Nichols, who combined older solstice and equinox observances with Celtic and Germanic festivals into one calendar. Many of its ingredients are old, but the full eight-point wheel isn't an unbroken ancient tradition, and the books above vary in how clearly they say so. The solstice itself is astronomy: it's the day the sun reaches its lowest point in the sky. That's a real, observable event, and marking it needs no belief at all.

Never leave a lit candle unattended, and keep evergreens and cloth well away from the flame.

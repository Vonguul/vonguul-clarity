Ostara marks the spring equinox, around March 20 in the Northern Hemisphere, when day and night are close to equal length. It's the festival after Imbolc on the Wheel of the Year, and the first one that actually feels like spring. The equinox is plain astronomy, which makes this an easy festival to approach even if you're unsure about the rest.

## Ostara at home, simply

- **Notice the balance.** Look up your local sunrise and sunset times and write them down. Check again a month later.
- **Plant something.** Start a few seeds on a windowsill. It's the most natural equinox activity there is, and you'll see results within weeks.
- **Press the first flowers.** Keep them with the date you found them, and compare next year.
- **Take stock.** Use the equal day and night as a prompt: what do you want more of this year, and what could you let go of?

If you marked Yule or Imbolc, this continues the same rhythm. See [the Wheel of the Year, starting with Yule](/articles/wheel-of-the-year-yule-for-beginners/), and use a [2027 moon and astrology calendar](/articles/2027-astrology-moon-planners-calendars/) if you want the dates on your wall.

## Where the name comes from

The trail is short. In the 8th century, the English monk Bede wrote that the month around April was named after a goddess called Eostre, and that feasts were held for her. That single passage is nearly all that's known about her. In 1835 Jacob Grimm proposed a German equivalent, Ostara, working backward from the old word for Easter. The modern festival took the name in 1974, when the Wiccan writer Aidan Kelly chose it for the spring equinox.

## Worth being direct about

Ostara isn't an unbroken ancient festival. The name is about fifty years old, and the eight-festival Wheel of the Year dates from the 1950s. You'll often read that Easter eggs and the Easter hare come from Eostre, but there's no good evidence for it. The hare link was a 19th-century suggestion, and eggs have their own Christian history. None of that stops the equinox being a good moment to pause. Treat the festival as a seasonal rhythm and a prompt for reflection, not a ritual with effects.
